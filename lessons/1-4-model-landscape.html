1.4 The model landscape and how models are made

"LLM" is one kind of model among several, and a chat model is the end of a long pipeline of training stages. This lesson maps the kinds of models you will meet, how a chat model is built, when you should change a model versus just change the request, and what size, open weights and licences mean in practice.

~35 minModule 1Concepts + Python

1The kinds of models you will meet

Most models in GenAI work today are built from the Transformer architecture (Stage 2 takes it apart). What differs is what they are trained to do and what comes out. Six kinds cover nearly everything you will use at work:

D
Decoder-only language models
Generate text token by token, exactly the loop from Lesson 1.2. GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, Mistral and Qwen models are of this kind. When people say "LLM", they almost always mean this.
E
Encoder-only models
Read a whole text at once and produce a representation of it, not new text. BERT (2018) is the classic example. Used for classification, named-entity extraction and as the basis of many embedding models.
ED
Encoder-decoder models
An encoder reads the input, a decoder writes the output. The original 2017 Transformer was built this way, for translation; T5 is a well-known later example. Still common for translation and some summarisation.
V
Embedding models
Turn a text (or an image) into a fixed-length list of numbers, a vector, so that similar meanings get nearby vectors. They power semantic search and RAG retrieval. Lesson 2.2 builds this idea from scratch.
∿
Diffusion models
Generate images (also audio and video) by starting from random noise and removing noise step by step. Stable Diffusion and most image generators work this way.
M
Multimodal models
Accept more than one kind of input, typically text plus images, sometimes audio, and some also produce images or speech. Most current frontier chat models are multimodal.

Two details matter

The first explains why encoders and decoders are good at different jobs. The second explains why image generation feels so different from text generation.

1
Encoders look both ways, decoders look only back

In Lesson 1.2 you met the causal mask: in a decoder, each position may only see the tokens before it, because it is trained to predict the next one. An encoder has no such mask. Every token sees every other token, before and after it.

So an encoder builds a better understanding of a text it already has, which is why it suits classification and embeddings. But it cannot generate text left to right, because it was never trained to predict what comes next. A decoder can generate, at the price of reading only one way. Large decoders have become so capable that they now also do many classic encoder jobs, but small encoder models are still much cheaper for high-volume classification and embedding.

2
Diffusion generates a whole image at once, many times over

A language model commits to one token at a time and never revisits it. A diffusion model is trained the other way round: take real images, add noise to them, and train a network to predict the noise so it can be removed. To generate, it starts from pure random noise and runs that denoising step many times. Every step refines the whole image a little, guided by your text prompt.

For an engineer, the practical differences are: image generation has no "tokens out" billing (you usually pay per image), outputs vary just as much as with text, and the same evaluation thinking applies (Lesson 1.3).

2How a chat model is made

Lesson 1.2 introduced pretraining and post-training in two sentences. Here is the full pipeline that turns raw text into the assistant you call through an API.

1
Pretraining: learn language and knowledge

The next-token objective from Lesson 1.2, run on an enormous text collection. Recent open models report training on more than ten trillion tokens (Meta, for example, reported over 15 trillion for Llama 3). This stage costs millions of GPU-hours and produces a base model: fluent, knowledgeable, but it only continues text.

It also fixes the knowledge cutoff: the model knows nothing published after its training data was collected, unless you supply it in the request.

2
Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): learn to answer

Training continues on a much smaller, carefully written set of (instruction, good answer) pairs, often tens of thousands to millions of examples. The loss is still next-token cross-entropy, but only on the answer part. The model learns the behaviour of an assistant: treat the text as a request, answer it, use the chat format with roles.

3
Preference tuning: learn which answers people prefer

Writing perfect answers is hard; comparing two answers is easy. So people (or a model following written principles) are shown two answers to the same prompt and pick the better one. Training then pushes the model toward the preferred kind of answer: more helpful, more honest about uncertainty, refusing harmful requests. The two main methods, RLHF and DPO, are explained in the Extra below.

4
Optional: reinforcement learning on checkable tasks

Since 2024, "reasoning" models add a stage where the model practises problems whose answers can be checked automatically, such as maths with known results or code with unit tests. Answers that turn out correct are rewarded. This teaches the model to write out longer intermediate reasoning before answering, which costs more output tokens per reply (Lesson 1.2: output tokens are the slow, expensive ones).

≈
Analogy from your own work

Pretraining is like a new analyst reading every report, wiki page and SQL script in the company for a year. SFT is their onboarding, where a senior colleague shows worked examples of good answers. Preference tuning is feedback on real work: "this version is clearer than that one". After that, your requests are the daily tasks they are given.

+RLHF and DPO, precisely Extra

Both start from the same data: prompts with two answers, one marked preferred (often written yw, "winner") and one rejected (yl, "loser").

1
RLHF: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

Step one trains a separate reward model, a network that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs a score, so that preferred answers score higher than rejected ones. Step two uses reinforcement learning (classically an algorithm called PPO): the chat model generates answers, the reward model scores them, and the chat model is updated to earn higher scores.

A penalty keeps the model close to its SFT version. Without it, the model learns to exploit quirks of the reward model ("reward hacking"), for example by producing long, flattering answers the reward model happens to like. This approach was made widely known by OpenAI's InstructGPT work in 2022, the basis of ChatGPT.

2
DPO: Direct Preference Optimization

Published in 2023, DPO showed that the same goal can be reached without a separate reward model and without reinforcement learning. It trains the chat model directly on the preference pairs with a loss that works much like a binary classifier: raise the probability of the preferred answer relative to the rejected one, again measured against the SFT version so the model does not drift too far.

It is simpler and cheaper to run, which is why it is popular for open-weight models and for companies doing their own preference tuning.

3Training, fine-tuning and inference: who changes what

The stages above belong to the model provider. Your options as an application team are different, and the key question is simple: does it change the weights?

ActivityChanges weights?Who does itCost

Fine-tuning means continuing training on your own examples, so the model's behaviour shifts in a lasting way. Full fine-tuning updates every weight. Parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA freeze the original weights and train a small set of extra ones, which is far cheaper and is what most companies actually do (Stage 6).

4The engineer's ladder: try the cheap fixes first

When a model does not do what you need, there is a standard order of things to try. Each rung costs more and is harder to undo than the one below it, so you climb only when the rung below has clearly failed.

1
Prompt and examples fix instructions and format

Most "the model is bad at this" problems are unclear requests. Clear instructions, a defined output format, and two or three worked examples in the request (few-shot prompting, Stage 3) fix a surprising share of them in minutes.

2
RAG and tools fix missing knowledge

If the model lacks facts (your documents, your data, anything after its cutoff), supply them in the request. Retrieval and tools keep the facts current, let you show sources, and respect permissions, because the application decides what each user's request may contain.

3
Fine-tuning fixes behaviour, not knowledge

Fine-tuning is the right tool when you need a consistent style or format across thousands of calls, a narrow task done well by a smaller and cheaper model, or a behaviour that is hard to describe in a prompt. It is a poor way to add facts: facts learned in training cannot be updated without retraining, cannot be cited, and can still be misremembered. Teams that fine-tune to "teach the model our documents" usually end up adding RAG anyway.

i
Engineering principle #3

Knowledge goes in the request (RAG, tools). Behaviour can go in the weights (fine-tuning), but only after prompting has been tried and measured. "Measured" means a test set from Lesson 1.3, not a feeling.

5Parameters: what model size means in practice

Definition: a model's parameters are its learned numbers, the weights adjusted during training. "An 8B model" has about 8 billion of them. More parameters can store more patterns and usually give better quality, but every one of them has to sit in memory and take part in computing every token.

Each parameter is stored as a number with a certain precision. Training usually uses 16-bit formats (fp16 or bf16, 2 bytes each). Quantisation stores the weights in fewer bits after training, such as 8-bit or 4-bit, which saves memory at some cost in quality. The memory just to hold the weights is simple arithmetic:

memory ≈ number of parameters × bytes per parameter
memory.py
# memory needed just to hold the weights (no KV cache, no activations)
bytes_per_param = {"fp32": 4, "fp16/bf16": 2, "int8": 1, "int4": 0.5}

for billions in (1, 8, 70):
    row = ", ".join(f"{name} {billions * b:g} GB" for name, b in bytes_per_param.items())
    print(f"{billions}B params: {row}")
Output · real run
1B params: fp32 4 GB, fp16/bf16 2 GB, int8 1 GB, int4 0.5 GB
8B params: fp32 32 GB, fp16/bf16 16 GB, int8 8 GB, int4 4 GB
70B params: fp32 280 GB, fp16/bf16 140 GB, int8 70 GB, int4 35 GB

The trick in the code: billions of parameters times bytes per parameter gives billions of bytes, which is gigabytes (using 1 GB = 109 bytes). The :g format drops pointless decimals, so 16.0 prints as 16.

Two details matter

1
Mixture-of-experts: total vs active parameters

Some models are split into many "expert" sub-networks, and for each token a small router picks only a few of them. Mistral's Mixtral 8x7B, for example, has about 47 billion parameters in total but uses about 13 billion for each token. So it needs the memory of a 47B model but computes each token about as fast as a 13B one. When a model card lists two numbers, total and active, this is why.

2
Parameter count and context window are unrelated

The context window (how many tokens fit in one request) is a design and training choice, not a consequence of size. A small model can have a long window and a large model a short one. Check both separately when choosing a model.

★
Why it matters at work

Bigger is not automatically better for your task. A small model, sometimes fine-tuned, often matches a large one on a narrow job like classification or extraction, at a fraction of the cost and latency. Model selection (Stage 3) means testing a few sizes on your own test set.

6Open weights, closed models and licences

Models reach you in one of two ways, and the choice shapes cost, privacy and how much control you have.

1
Closed (hosted) models

You call the provider's API and never see the weights. Examples: OpenAI's GPT models, Anthropic's Claude, Google's Gemini. Usually the highest quality available, with no infrastructure to run. In exchange your data goes to the provider (under contract), you pay per token, and the provider decides when a model version is retired.

2
Open-weight models

The weights can be downloaded, so you can run the model on your own servers or cloud account and fine-tune it. Examples: Meta's Llama, Mistral, Qwen and Gemma families. Data can stay inside your network, cost at high volume can be lower, and the version never changes unless you change it. In exchange you run GPUs, serving software and monitoring yourself, and the best open models usually trail the best closed ones somewhat.

!
Open weights is not the same as open source

Most "open" models publish weights but not their training data or full training code. The Open Source Initiative's definition of open-source AI (2024) asks for much more than weights. More importantly for you: read the licence. Some models use permissive licences such as Apache 2.0 (Mistral 7B, for example). Others use custom licences with conditions, such as Meta's Llama licence, which adds rules on use and on very large companies. Your legal or procurement team decides what is allowed.

Model cards: read before you choose

A model card is the documentation published with a model, an idea proposed by Google researchers in 2019. A good one tells you:

  • Intended uses, and uses the authors advise against.
  • Training data, at least in summary, and the knowledge cutoff.
  • Context window, languages supported, parameter count (total and active).
  • Evaluation results, and known limitations and biases.
  • The licence.

Treat its benchmark numbers as a first filter only. The deciding test is always your own test set, on your own data (Lesson 1.3).

?Check yourself

Q1You need to tag 5 million support tickets a day into 20 categories. Why might an encoder model beat a large chat LLM here?

The task is understanding a text that already exists, not generating new text, which is what encoders are built for. A small fine-tuned encoder is far cheaper and faster per ticket, gives a fixed label instead of free text, and at 5 million a day the per-token cost of a large LLM adds up quickly. Test both on a labelled sample before deciding.

Q2What does SFT teach that pretraining does not, and what does preference tuning add on top?

Pretraining teaches language and knowledge but only to continue text. SFT teaches assistant behaviour: treat input as a request and answer it in the chat format. Preference tuning then shifts the model toward the answers people prefer among several acceptable ones: more helpful, more honest, safer.

Q3A team wants to fine-tune a model on the company handbook so it "knows the policies". What do you recommend, and why?

RAG over the handbook instead. Policies change, answers should cite the exact section, and different users may be allowed to see different documents. Fine-tuned facts cannot be updated without retraining, cannot be cited and can be misremembered. Fine-tuning could be added later for tone or format, if a test set shows prompting is not enough.

Q4Roughly how much memory do the weights of a 32B model need at bf16 and at 4-bit?

32 × 2 = 64 GB at bf16, and 32 × 0.5 = 16 GB at 4-bit. Running it needs extra memory on top for the KV cache and activations.

Q5A model card says "141B total parameters, 39B active". What does that tell you about memory and speed?

It is a mixture-of-experts model. Memory must hold all 141B parameters, but each token only uses about 39B, so per-token compute is closer to a 39B dense model.

Q6Give two reasons a regulated company might choose an open-weight model despite slightly lower quality.

Any two of: data never leaves its own network; the model version stays fixed, which helps validation and audit; cost at very high volume; freedom to fine-tune; no dependency on a provider retiring a model. The licence still has to be approved.

What to retain from this lesson

A chat LLM is one kind of model, produced by a pipeline of pretraining, supervised fine-tuning and preference tuning. As an application engineer you mostly change the request, sometimes fine-tune, and practically never pretrain.

  • Kinds of models: decoder LLMs generate, encoders understand, embedding models produce vectors for search, diffusion models denoise into images, multimodal models mix inputs.
  • Encoders see both directions; decoders see only the past, which is what lets them generate.
  • Pretraining (next token, trillions of tokens) → base model → SFT → preference tuning (RLHF or DPO) → chat model; reasoning models add RL on checkable tasks.
  • The ladder: prompt, examples, RAG and tools, then fine-tuning. Knowledge in the request, behaviour in the weights.
  • Weight memory ≈ parameters × bytes per parameter; quantisation trades quality for memory; MoE models have total and active counts.
  • Closed vs open weights trades quality and convenience against control and data location. Always check the licence and the model card.
